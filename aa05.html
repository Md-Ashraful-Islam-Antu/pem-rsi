📜 Project Contracts ★★★☆
A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties. In project management, contracts define the scope of work, deliverables, timeline, payment terms, and responsibilities.

💵 Project Financing ★★★☆
Project financing is the method of funding a project where repayment depends on the project's own cash flows and assets — not on the balance sheet of the sponsors (the parent company).

Conventional Finance vs Project Finance

DimensionConventional FinanceProject Finance
BorrowerThe corporation (parent company)Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) set up for project
Repayment fromAll corporate cash flowsOnly project's own cash flows
CollateralCompany's total assetsProject assets only
Balance sheet effectAppears on sponsor's balance sheet (on-balance sheet)Off-balance sheet — does not affect sponsors' credit rating
RiskSponsor bears all riskRisk shared among lenders, sponsors, and other parties
Used forSmall/routine investmentsLarge infrastructure (oil, gas, power, toll roads)

Q1. What is project financing? How does it differ from conventional financing? [5–8 marks, ★★★]

Answer:
Project Financing is a method of funding where the debt and equity used to finance the project are paid back from the cash flow generated by the project itself — not from the sponsor's general assets.

Key features of project financing:
• A Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) or Special Purpose Entity (SPE) is created specifically for the project
• The project's assets, rights, and cash flows are held by the SPV
• Lenders have limited recourse — they can only claim the project assets, not the sponsor's other assets
• Off-balance sheet for sponsors — doesn't appear on sponsor's balance sheet
• Risk is shared among sponsors, lenders, contractors, and government

Comparison with Conventional Financing:
AspectProject FinancingConventional Financing
Repayment sourceProject's own cash flowsSponsor's general assets
RecourseLimited or non-recourseFull recourse to sponsor
Balance sheetOff-balance sheetOn-balance sheet
RiskShared among many partiesBorne mainly by sponsor
Used forLarge infrastructure, power plants, toll roadsNormal business assets, working capital

Q2. Classify and explain sources of project financing. [8–10 marks, ★★★]

Answer:
Sources of project financing are classified into three main categories:

A. Equity (Ownership) Sources:
1. Ordinary (Common) Shares — Voting rights; dividend not guaranteed; highest risk for investor
2. Preference Shares — Fixed dividend; preference over ordinary in liquidation; no voting right
3. Internal Accruals (Retained Earnings) — Profits plowed back; no interest cost; cheapest source

B. Debt (Borrowed) Sources:
1. Debentures / Bonds — Long-term debt; fixed interest; secured or unsecured
2. Term Loans — From banks/DFIs; repaid in installments over project life
3. Syndicated Loans — Multiple banks share one large loan (reduces single-bank risk)
4. Bridge Financing — Short-term interim loan until permanent financing is arranged
5. Unsecured Loans — No collateral; higher interest; from parent companies or related parties

C. Miscellaneous Sources:
1. Deferred Credit — Supplier/vendor provides goods now and collects payment later (trade credit)
2. Leasing / Hire Purchase — Use asset without owning it; payment over time; preserves capital

Q3. Differentiate between Turnkey and Non-Turnkey contracts. [5–8 marks, ★★★]

Answer:
Turnkey Contract: The contractor is responsible for the complete project — design, procurement, construction, testing, and commissioning. The client simply "turns the key" and the facility is ready to operate. Price is fixed upfront.

Non-Turnkey Contract: The client manages the project and separately contracts for design, construction, equipment, etc. The client retains more control but also bears more risk and coordination burden.

AspectTurnkeyNon-Turnkey
Client controlLow — contractor manages allHigh — client manages phases
Client riskLow — fixed price, single responsibilityHigh — coordinates multiple contractors
Price certaintyHigh — lump sumLower — costs can escalate
Best whenClient lacks technical expertiseClient wants maximum flexibility and control

Q4. List and explain the essentials of a valid contract. [5–7 marks, ★★]

Answer:
A contract is a legally binding agreement. For it to be valid, the following essentials must be present:

1. Offer: One party makes a definite proposal to another.
2. Acceptance: The other party unconditionally agrees to the offer (counter-offer = new offer).
3. Consideration: Something of value must be exchanged (money, goods, services). A contract without consideration is a gift, not a contract.
4. Capacity: Both parties must be legally competent — adults, of sound mind, not under duress.
5. Legality of Object: The contract's purpose must be legal — cannot enforce a contract to commit a crime.
6. Mutual Assent (Consensus ad idem): Both parties must mean the same thing — no misunderstanding about the subject matter.
7. Writing (where required): Some contracts (land sale, insurance) must be in writing to be enforceable.

If any essential is missing, the contract is void or voidable.
