Chapter 12 covers how the PM monitors and controls project progress. The focus is on designing a monitoring system (what to track, when to measure, how to collect data) and responding to deviations with the right control mechanism.

Exam focus: Name and explain the 4 monitoring policies with diagrams. Know the 3 types of cybernetic control. Know the 5 data collection methods. Know the 4 sections of a post-control report.
👁️ 4 Monitoring Policies ★★★★ — 6× in PYQ
Most frequently asked topic in this chapter. You must know all 4 types, draw the interval diagrams, and explain when each is appropriate.
A monitoring policy defines when and how often to check on project progress. The choice depends on how much risk exists at different phases of the project.
#PolicyWhen to MonitorBest Used WhenDrawback
1 At Random Times Unannounced, irregular check-ins Preventing complacency; keeps team alert (they can't prepare only for scheduled checks) Unpredictable; hard to plan corrective action; may miss critical periods
2 Equal Interval Fixed schedule: weekly, monthly Routine projects with uniform risk throughout lifecycle; easy to plan Doesn't adapt to risk — a weekly check may be too frequent early, too sparse near deadline
3 More Frequent at Start Denser early, sparse later Projects with high front-end risk (new technology, first-time team, complex requirements); early problems compound if uncaught Relaxes oversight near end when deadline pressure is highest
4 More Frequent at End Sparse early, denser later Projects where end-phase has highest risk (integration, testing, commissioning); final deliverables need tight control Early-phase problems may go undetected
Most common in practice: Policy 2 (equal interval) for routine projects. Policy 3 (denser at start) for innovative/R&D projects. Policy 4 (denser at end) for deadline-critical projects.
📊 5 Data Collection Methods ★★★★
These are the methods used to measure project performance during monitoring. Each method suits different types of project metrics.
#MethodHow It WorksBest ForExample
1 Frequency Count Count how many times an event occurs Discrete events, defects, occurrences Number of defects per batch; number of change requests per week
2 Raw Numbers Measure the actual numeric value Quantifiable continuous data Actual cost spent ($12,500); days elapsed; lines of code written
3 Subjective Numeric Ratings Rate on a numeric scale based on judgment Qualitative factors that need quantification Team morale rated 7/10; customer satisfaction 8/10; risk level 4/5
4 Indicators Measure a proxy variable that signals status When direct measurement is costly or impractical % milestones completed on time (indicator of schedule health); staff turnover rate (indicator of team health)
5 Verbal Measures Descriptive assessment (Good/Fair/Poor, Red/Yellow/Green) Quick status summaries, executive reporting Project status: "On track" / "At risk" / "Critical"; stoplight reports
⚙️ 3 Control Mechanisms ★★★★ — 6× in PYQ
Equally high-frequency with monitoring policies. Know the 3 types (Cybernetic with its 3 orders, Go/No-Go, Post Control) and their differences.

1. Cybernetic Control (3 Orders)

Cybernetic control uses a feedback loop — the system measures actual performance, compares it to the plan, and self-corrects. It has three escalating orders based on what changes in response.
OrderWhat ChangesWhat Stays FixedExample
1st Order
(Basic feedback)		 Actions/outputs are adjusted to bring performance back to target Goal and structure are fixed Spending is over budget → cut discretionary costs to get back on track
2nd Order
(Adaptive control)		 Goal or standard itself is adjusted when it's clear the original target was wrong Structure/organization is fixed Original budget estimate was unrealistic → revise budget baseline to reflect new reality
3rd Order
(Meta-control)		 The control process itself is restructured; changing the system of control Nothing — entire system is redesigned Monitoring system itself is not catching problems early → redesign the entire project reporting and control process

2. Go/No-Go Control

Go/No-Go control is a milestone-based binary check. At predefined checkpoints, the project is evaluated against specific criteria. If criteria are met → continue (Go). If not → stop or redesign (No-Go). This is used for high-risk decisions with large resource commitments.

3. Post Control

Post control (also called postmortem control) happens after the project is complete. It evaluates what went right and wrong to improve future projects. It is NOT used to control the current project (which is already done) but to build organizational learning.
📋 Post Control Report — 4 Sections ★★★☆
A Post Control (Postmortem) Report is written after project completion to capture lessons learned. It has four standard sections.
#SectionContentPurpose
1 Objectives What were the original goals? Were they achieved? If not, why not? Evaluate overall project success against initial intent
2 Milestones / Checkpoints / Budget Detailed review of schedule adherence, checkpoint status, budget vs actual Understand where and why deviations occurred throughout the project
3 Final Report Summary of outcomes, deliverables status, stakeholder satisfaction Document the project's final state for organizational records
4 Recommendations What should be done differently on future projects? What practices should be retained? Transfer knowledge to future PMs; improve organizational capability
Key distinction: Post control is not "control" in the traditional sense — the project is over and you can't change its outcome. It is organizational learning control — controlling the quality of the organization's future projects by codifying what was learned.
