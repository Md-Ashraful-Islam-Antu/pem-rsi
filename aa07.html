💰 Budgeting Methods ★★★☆
Sir's key insight: "Finance is the Heart of the economy — just as the heart pumps blood, finance circulates money through the economic body. For a project, the budget is its heartbeat." Top-down = Autocracy; Bottom-up = Democracy.
There are three main approaches to setting a project budget. Each has different accuracy levels and organizational requirements.
MethodDirectionHow It WorksProsCons
Top-Down Senior mgmt → Team Senior management sets the total budget based on past projects, analogies, or affordability. Teams are given their allocations. Fast; strategic alignment; overall budget known early May be unrealistic; ignores actual work details; demotivates team
Bottom-Up Team → Senior mgmt Teams estimate each work package individually; estimates are rolled up to get the total project budget. Accurate; team buy-in; based on real work Slow; teams may pad estimates; may exceed affordability
Iterative Negotiation Combines both: initial top-down constraint, then bottom-up detailing, followed by negotiation until agreed. Realistic and practical; best compromise Time-consuming; requires skilled negotiators

Types of Project Costs (Sakib Notes)

Cost TypeDescriptionExamples
Direct Cost Directly attributable to a specific project activity or work package. Can be charged directly to the project. Labor wages for project staff, raw materials, equipment purchased for the project
Overhead (Indirect) Cannot be directly charged to one project — shared across multiple projects or the whole organization. Allocated proportionally. Office rent, utilities, shared admin staff, common equipment depreciation
G&A (General & Administrative) Company-level costs that support all operations. Applied as a percentage rate to all projects. Top management salaries, corporate legal, finance department, company-wide IT

📊 Time-Phased Budget (3 Curves) ★★★★ — 7× in PYQ
Most asked budgeting topic. Draw the 3 curves accurately on a single graph. Know what each curve represents and what their relationships tell the PM.
A time-phased budget shows how costs accumulate over the project timeline. Three curves are plotted: Committed, Scheduled, and Actual costs.

The Three Curves Explained

CurveWhat It TracksWhen RecordedSignificance
Committed Cost Costs committed (orders placed, contracts signed) — obligations made even if not yet paid When purchase orders or contracts are signed Shows financial obligations; rises earliest; "point of no return" for spending
Scheduled Cost Costs planned to be spent per the schedule (Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled — BCWS) Created during planning; baseline Baseline for comparison; also called the "cost baseline" or S-curve
Actual Cost Actual cash outflows — money actually spent on work completed As work is done and invoices paid Compared with scheduled to identify cost variances; below scheduled = under budget
Reading the chart:
• If Actual < Scheduled → Under budget (good)
• If Actual > Scheduled → Over budget (bad — take corrective action)
• Committed always rises first (you commit before you spend)
• The gap between Committed and Actual represents liabilities not yet paid
📉 Learning Curve ★★★★
Important numerical topic. Know the formula, what learning rate means, and how to calculate initial time given expert time and learning rate.
The learning curve (also called experience curve) states that as workers gain experience doing a repetitive task, the time to complete it decreases at a predictable rate. Each time cumulative production doubles, the unit time decreases by a fixed percentage.
Learning Curve Formula
Tn = T1 · nr
Where: Tn = time for the n-th unit  |  T1 = time for the 1st unit
n = unit number  |  r = learning exponent = log(learning rate) / log(2)
Learning Exponent r
r = log(learning rate) / log(2)
For 80% learning rate: r = log(0.80) / log(2) = −0.0969 / 0.3010 = −0.322
Note: r is negative (time decreases as n increases)

Why Learning Curve Matters for PM

Critical insight: If the PM ignores the learning curve and assumes all units take the same time (T₁), the budget for later units will be overestimated. But if the PM doesn't account for the initial slow period (high T₁), the early-project budget will be underestimated. Proper learning curve use prevents both errors.
Learning RateMeaningExponent r
80%Every doubling of production → 20% reduction in unit timer = −0.322
90%Every doubling → 10% reduction in unit timer = −0.152
100%No learning; time stays constantr = 0
⚠️ Estimating Errors (MAD, MAR, TS) ★★★☆
These statistical measures help evaluate the quality of budget estimates by comparing actual values to forecasted values over time.
Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)
MAD = Σ|A(t) − F(t)| / n
Average of the absolute differences between actual A(t) and forecast F(t). Measures average magnitude of error regardless of direction.
Mean Absolute Rate (MAR)
MAR = Σ|A(t)/F(t) − 1| / n × 100%
Average relative error as a percentage. Useful for comparing across projects of different scales.
Tracking Signal (TS)
TS = RSFE / MAD
RSFE = Running Sum of Forecast Errors = Σ[A(t) − F(t)]
TS tells you if errors are biased in one direction. A TS consistently positive means estimates are systematically too low.
Rule of thumb: |TS| > 4 indicates a biased forecast — revise the model.
🔢 Learning Curve Numericals ★★★★

Problem 1: Find Initial Time Given Expert Time

An expert worker can complete a job in 50 hours after doing it 20 times. The learning rate is 80%. What was the time for the very first unit?
Solution
Tn = T1 · nr
r = log(0.80)/log(2) = −0.322
50 = T₁ × (20)^(−0.322)
(20)^(−0.322) = e^(−0.322 × ln20) = e^(−0.322 × 2.996) = e^(−0.965) = 0.381
50 = T₁ × 0.381
T₁ = 50 / 0.381 = 131.2 hours

Problem 2: Time for nth Unit

T₁ = 100 hours, learning rate = 80%. What is the time for the 8th unit?
Solution
r = log(0.80)/log(2) = −0.322
T₈ = 100 × (8)^(−0.322)
(8)^(−0.322) = e^(−0.322 × ln8) = e^(−0.322 × 2.079) = e^(−0.670) = 0.512
T₈ = 100 × 0.512 = 51.2 hours
Verification: At n=1, time=100. At n=2, time=80 (80% of 100). At n=4, time=64 (80% of 80). At n=8, time=51.2 (80% of 64). ✓

Q1. Draw and explain the time-phased budget with 3 curves. [8–10 marks, ★★★★]

Answer:
A time-phased budget shows three curves plotted against time on the x-axis and cumulative cost on the y-axis:

Curve 1 — Committed Cost (highest, rises first): This represents orders placed and contracts signed — the financial commitments the project has made. It rises steeply early when materials are ordered and contracts are awarded. At project end, committed = total budget.

Curve 2 — Scheduled Cost / Budgeted Cost of Work Scheduled (BCWS): The planned S-curve — how the budget was originally spread over time based on the schedule. This is the baseline. It has a classic S-shape: slow start, rapid middle, slow finish.

Curve 3 — Actual Cost (may be above or below scheduled): Real cash expenditures as work proceeds. Comparing actual to scheduled gives the cost variance. If actual is above scheduled → over budget; below → under budget (or ahead of schedule).

[Draw axes: x = Time (project start to end), y = Cumulative Cost (0 to Total Budget). Draw 3 S-curves. Committed rises first and fastest. Scheduled is the middle S-curve baseline. Actual may diverge above or below Scheduled. Label all three clearly.]

Key insight: The gap between Committed and Scheduled represents obligations not yet paid. The gap between Actual and Scheduled shows whether the project is over/under budget.

Q2. Learning curve numerical — find T₁ given Tₙ, n, and learning rate. [8–10 marks, ★★★★]

Formula: Tₙ = T₁ × nʳ    where r = log(learning rate) / log(2)

Problem: The 8th unit takes 50 hours. The learning rate is 80%. Find the time for the 1st unit (T₁).

Step 1 — Find r:
r = log(0.80) / log(2) = (−0.09691) / (0.30103) = −0.3219

Step 2 — Use formula Tₙ = T₁ × nʳ:
50 = T₁ × 8^(−0.3219)

Step 3 — Calculate 8^(−0.3219):
8^(−0.3219) = 1 / 8^(0.3219) = 1 / 2.0 = 0.5120
(At 80% learning rate, every doubling of quantity reduces time to 80%, so T₁ → 0.8T₁ at n=2, → 0.64T₁ at n=4, → 0.512T₁ at n=8)

Step 4 — Solve for T₁:
50 = T₁ × 0.512
T₁ = 50 / 0.512 = 97.66 ≈ 97.7 hours

Verification: T₁=97.7 → T₂=97.7×0.8=78.1 → T₄=78.1×0.8=62.5 → T₈=62.5×0.8=50 ✓

Q3. What happens if the learning curve is ignored in budgeting? [5 marks, ★★★]

Answer:
If the learning curve is ignored and the estimator assumes the first unit time (T₁) for all subsequent units, the budget will be significantly overestimated.

Consequence 1 — Overestimated budget: More money is allocated than needed. The project appears more expensive than it truly is, potentially causing it to be rejected in appraisal even though it would be viable.

Consequence 2 — Competitive loss: If two firms are bidding, the firm that accounts for learning will bid lower and win. The firm that ignores learning will overbid and lose contracts.

Consequence 3 — Inefficient resource allocation: Labor budgets are set too high early in production runs. This leads to overstaffing or wasted budget that could be used elsewhere.

Consequence 4 — Misleading performance metrics: Actual costs will appear much lower than budgeted (a false positive). Management may not realize learning is occurring and will not plan production runs optimally.

Q4. Explain top-down, bottom-up, and iterative budgeting. [5–8 marks, ★★★]

Top-Down Budgeting: Senior management sets the overall budget based on past experience and intuition, then allocates it down to departments and work packages. Fast to prepare, but may not reflect actual work requirements. Team feels the budget is imposed.

Bottom-Up Budgeting: Each work package manager estimates costs at the lowest level, then estimates are rolled up to get the total. Accurate and realistic, but time-consuming and may ignore organizational constraints.

Iterative Budgeting (Best Practice): Combines both. Management sets a top-down constraint (ceiling). Teams do bottom-up estimates. If estimates exceed ceiling, negotiation occurs — scope is reduced, efficiency gains identified, or budget ceiling is raised with justification. The result is both realistic and affordable.

Most organizations use iterative budgeting in practice.
