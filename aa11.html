Crashing is the process of compressing a project's schedule by adding resources (at extra cost) to critical path activities. The goal is to find the cheapest way to meet an earlier deadline.

Exam focus: Given a network with normal and crash time/cost, reduce duration step by step (always crash the cheapest critical activity). Show each stage's total project cost. This carries the most marks (15–22).
📚 Crashing Concepts ★★★☆
Sir's definition: "Usually crashing মানে ভাঙতে ভাঙতে শেষ পর্যন্ত নিয়ে আসা" (Usually crashing means bringing something to its end by pushing hard/breaking)
In project management: using extra resources to complete the project faster.

The 3 Things in Every Project (PM Triangle)

Performance ✦ Time ✦ Budget

Performance cannot be compromised. If by some reason time gets delayed, we must sacrifice money to recover the time.

Sir's example: Miss the BUET bus → take Uber to attend class. You spend extra money (Uber fare) to recover the lost time. Same logic in projects!

When Does Crashing Happen?

Two situations:

  1. Project gets delayed by chance (unexpected delays)
  2. Customer demands finishing before the original deadline

In both cases: use extra resources = Project Crashing

Resources Used in Crashing

  1. Overtime — existing workers work extra hours
  2. Special equipment — faster/better machinery
  3. Additional staff — hire more workers
Crashing requires careful planning — you're spending extra. That's why the PM needs negotiation power.

Crashing Rules (Sir's key points)

  • Always work on the critical path — only crash activities that are on the critical path. Crashing non-critical activities wastes money without reducing project duration.
  • First draw the network with normal time, then do scaling
  • When CKT (circuit/network) is not complete → add dummy arrows to close it
  • Among critical path activities, crash the cheapest one first (lowest cost slope)
Crashing means reducing a project's duration by adding resources (more workers, overtime, better equipment) to activities on the critical path. This always increases direct costs but may reduce indirect costs (overhead, penalties).
Cost Slope Formula
Cost Slope = (CC − NC) / (NT − CT)
CC = Crash Cost (cost at maximum compression)
NC = Normal Cost (cost at normal duration)
NT = Normal Time (baseline duration)
CT = Crash Time (minimum possible duration)
Cost Slope = extra cost per day of reduction
Note: denominator is (NT − CT) because you're REDUCING time (NT > CT)
ConceptExplanation
Normal pointNormal time and cost — baseline conditions
Crash pointMinimum possible time and maximum direct cost
Direct costsLabor, materials — rise when you crash (overtime, extra workers)
Indirect costsOverhead, penalties — fall when you shorten the project
Optimal durationWhere total cost (direct + indirect) is minimized
Partial crashingReducing by less than the full crash amount (staying between normal and crash point)
🔧 Step-by-Step Crashing Procedure ★★★★
Always follow this procedure for crashing numericals.
StepAction
1Draw the network, find the critical path, calculate project duration
2Calculate cost slope for each activity = (CC−NC)/(NT−CT)
3Identify all activities on the critical path
4Among critical path activities, choose the one with the lowest cost slope
5Crash that activity by 1 day (or as many days as possible within its crash limit)
6Recalculate the network — the critical path may change!
7Record the new project duration and total cost
8Repeat from Step 3 until desired duration is reached
Critical: When you crash, the critical path may change.
• A new critical path may emerge (a previously non-critical path may become critical)
• When two paths become critical simultaneously, you must crash BOTH paths together
• Crashing stops when: (a) desired duration reached, or (b) no more crashing is cost-effective (direct cost gain < indirect cost saved)
Two Principles of Crashing:
1. Always crash on the critical path — crashing non-critical activities wastes money without reducing project duration
2. Among critical path activities, always crash the cheapest (lowest cost slope) first

Options on a-d: d (30 USD/day).

To reduce 1 day: crash e* by 2 days (70 USD) + crash d by 1 day (30 USD) = 100 USD
BUT e* crashes 2 days → goes from 6 days to 4 days skipping 5 days. This creates a problem.

Alternative: Don't crash b, don't crash e, just crash d by 1 day:
d: 4→3 days. a-d path = 2+3 = 5 days. a-b-e path = 2+1+3 = 6 days → still 6 days critical.
Crash e* by 2 days: a-b-e = 2+1+1 = 4 days (but that's too much).

Actual solution from Sir's notes: Since e* must crash fully (2 days), and doing e* + d gives:
e*: 3→1 (extra = 70 USD), d: 4→3 (extra = 30 USD) = 100 extra. But result = 4 days duration (not 5).
Or without b crashed: Crash e* alone: a-b-e = 2+2+1 = 5. a-d = 2+4 = 6 → need also d.

New cost = 220 + 100 = 320 USD. Duration: 5 days (a-b-e = 2+2+1=5; a-d = 2+3=5; both still critical)

Revised cost with optimal crashing: 220+100−60 = 260 USD for 5 days. ✅

Now paths: a-c (2+2=4?), a-b-e, a-d all become critical at some point.

❓ Past Year Questions & Full Answers — AA Ch 11
The full crashing numerical (★★★★★) is the highest-marks question in the course. Master the stage-by-stage procedure completely.

Q1. Full crashing procedure — Step-by-step method. [15–22 marks, ★★★★★]

Setup: Given a network with Normal Time (NT), Normal Cost (NC), Crash Time (CT), Crash Cost (CC) for each activity. Find the schedule at target duration with minimum total cost.

Step 1 — Calculate Cost Slope for each activity:
Cost Slope = (CC − NC) / (NT − CT) [cost per day of time reduction]

Step 2 — Perform CPM to find the critical path at normal durations.

Step 3 — Identify the cheapest activity on the critical path (lowest cost slope that still has crash days available).

Step 4 — Crash that activity by 1 day (or as many days as needed until: (a) the target duration is reached, or (b) another path becomes critical, or (c) the activity is fully crashed).

Step 5 — Update total cost: add (days crashed × cost slope). Update the schedule. Re-draw the network if needed.

Step 6 — Re-identify the critical path(s). If two paths are now critical, must crash one activity on EACH path, or one activity common to both paths.

Step 7 — Repeat Steps 3–6 until the target duration is reached.

Important rules:
• Never crash a non-critical activity (wastes money, doesn't reduce project duration)
• When two paths are critical, choose the cheapest combination that reduces BOTH paths
• A single activity that lies on both paths reduces both simultaneously (most efficient)

Q2. Calculate cost slopes for given activities. [5 marks, ★★★★]

Formula: Cost Slope = (Crash Cost − Normal Cost) / (Normal Time − Crash Time)

Q3. What is project crashing? State the 2 principles. [5 marks, ★★★]

Answer:
Project Crashing is the deliberate reduction of project duration by adding extra resources (overtime, additional workers, faster equipment) to critical activities — at additional cost. It is a time-cost trade-off decision.

Principle 1 — Crash only critical path activities: Only activities on the critical path control project duration. Spending money to shorten a non-critical activity is wasted — it won't reduce project duration until that path also becomes critical.

Principle 2 — Crash the cheapest critical activity first: Among all crashable critical activities, start with the one with the lowest cost slope (cost per day saved). This minimizes total crashing cost for a given time reduction.

Q4. When must you crash two paths simultaneously? [3–5 marks, ★★★]

Answer:
You must crash two paths simultaneously when two paths become critical at the same time during the crashing process.

This happens when a non-critical path, after the critical path has been shortened in earlier stages, catches up and also has zero slack — both paths now have the same duration.

At that point, crashing only one path will shorten IT, but the other (now equally long) path will still determine the project duration. So you must reduce BOTH paths together.

Options for crashing two critical paths:
1. Find an activity that lies on BOTH paths (crashes both with one decision — most efficient)
2. Crash one activity on each path (two separate crashes)
